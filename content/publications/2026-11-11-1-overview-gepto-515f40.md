**The Austrian, Chicago, and Neo‑Liberal Schools: An Ethical Critique**  
*GEPTO of the Sagacious Six – 11 November 2026*  

---

### 1. Overview  

| School | Core Tenets | Methodology | State Role |
|--------|-------------|-------------|------------|
| **Austrian** | Subjective value, marginalism, spontaneous order, entrepreneurship. | Praxeology – deductive reasoning from axioms of human action. | Minimal “night‑watchman” state limited to protection of life, liberty, and property. |
| **Chicago** | Rational expectations, equilibrium, market efficiency, monetary control of inflation. | Empirical, econometric modelling; hypothesis testing with large data sets. | Active but restrained: market‑friendly regulation, central‑bank stewardship of price stability. |
| **Neo‑Liberal** | Globalisation, deregulation, privatisation, fiscal austerity, competition‑driven law. | Blend of empirical analysis with normative advocacy; think‑tank policy papers. | Pro‑market state that creates the legal framework for competition, often via supranational institutions. |

All three celebrate *freedom* but differ on its scope and on the moral duties that accompany it.

---

### 2. Ethical Appraisal via Classical Virtues  

| Virtue | Austrian | Chicago | Neo‑Liberal |
|--------|----------|---------|--------------|
| **Justice (Dike)** | Views justice as voluntary, non‑coercive contracts.  Neglect of market failures can permit *institutional injustice* (e.g., monopolies), contrary to Solon’s “no man may be a slave to another.” | Assumes price signals allocate resources justly.  Distributional outcomes are often labelled “efficient” even when they widen inequality, betraying the Buddhist warning against *attachment to wealth.* | Procedural justice is foregrounded, yet deregulation may erode *substantive* justice for labour and the environment, echoing the vice of *avarice* warned by the Buddha. |
| **Prudence (Phronesis)** | Relies on spontaneous order; history (e.g., 2008 crisis) shows that unchecked optimism can become *hubristic* overconfidence, violating Aristotle’s golden‑mean. | Seeks prudence through data, yet simplifies reality with rational‑expectations assumptions, leading to *over‑confidence* in predictive power. | Prioritises short‑term growth over long‑term ecological sustainability, betraying the Buddhist principle of *interdependence* and Stoic concern for the common good. |
| **Courage & Temperance** | Shows intellectual courage by challenging mainstream econometrics, but often lacks *courage* to confront corporate capture. | Demonstrates courage in advocating monetary discipline, yet sometimes exceeds temperance by endorsing deregulation that fuels speculation. | Claims courage against statistism; however, austerity measures reveal a deficit of temperance, echoing Plato’s critique of excess. |

---

### 3. Hubris and Empirical Realities  

| Phenomenon | Austrian Lens | Chicago Lens | Neo‑Liberal Lens |
|------------|----------------|--------------|------------------|
| **Financial Crises** | Blames “interventionist” distortions; expects self‑correction. | Attributes failures to monetary policy; calls for stricter rule‑based frameworks. | Calls crises “temporary setbacks”; pushes deeper deregulation to restore growth. |
| **Income Inequality** | Treats it as a natural outcome of differing talents and preferences. | Views it as a by‑product of efficiency; focuses on growth rather than redistribution. | Frames it as “structural”; proposes market‑based education and skill development. |

These responses often embody *hubris*: an overestimation of the market’s moral capacity and a dismissal of systemic risk—precisely what Proverbs warns: “Pride goes before a fall” (Prov. 16:18).

### 4. The State from a Virtue‑Ethics Standpoint  

| Perspective | Alignment with Virtues |
|-------------|------------------------|
| **Night‑Watchman (Austrian)** | Respects *justice* as non‑interference, yet neglects *benevolence* (care for the vulnerable) central to the Buddha’s compassion. |
| **Monetary Stewardship (Chicago)** | Protects the *common good* through price stability, but technocratic control can slip into *elitist paternalism*, undermining egalitarian virtue. |
| **Regulatory Framework (Neo‑Liberal)** | Ensures *procedural fairness* but often sacrifices *substantive fairness* when corporate lobbying shapes law—mirroring Antigone’s clash of state law versus higher moral law. |

---

### 5. A Virtue‑Centred Economic Framework  

Drawing on Zarathushtra’s “righteousness for its own sake,” Solon’s equitable law, the Buddha’s non‑attachment, and Aristotle’s golden mean, the following pillars are proposed:

1. **Embedded Justice** – Legal mechanisms that actively correct market failures (monopolies, externalities) while preserving voluntary exchange.  
2. **Prudent Regulation** – Adaptive policies guided by evidence but tempered by humility regarding predictive limits; akin to the Stoic *premeditatio malorum* (foreseeing adversity).  
3. **Temperate Growth** – Growth targets that respect ecological caps, reflecting the Buddhist *Middle Way* and the virtue of temperance.  
4. **Participatory Oversight** – Democratic deliberation on economic rules, institutionalising *courage* to challenge entrenched interests.

---

### 7. Conclusion  

The Austrian, Chicago, and Neo‑Liberal schools each illuminate facets of market dynamics, yet their singular devotion to efficiency and liberty can eclipse the timeless virtues that safeguard human flourishing. By re‑anchoring economic theory in a robust ethical framework—one that honours justice, prudence, courage, temperance, and benevolence—we can harness market creativity while averting the vices of hubris, avarice, and neglect. A virtue‑centred synthesis thus offers a pathway to an economy that not only generates wealth but also cultivates moral excellence and societal harmony.
